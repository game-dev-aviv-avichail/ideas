## Question 2 – Children's Games

Three computer game ideas that borrow the mechanics and the feel of games kids play away from screens, then turn them into something new.

### 1. Rule Breakers

**Inspired by:** playground games where kids make up the rules and change them mid-game.

**The game:** A strategy game. You're in an arena with a few opponents, and you need to grab all the coins and reach the exit. The twist is that you can change the arena's rules, but every rule you turn on applies to everyone, you included. One rule might ban stepping on red tiles. Another might limit everyone to two steps per turn. Another might flip everyone's direction of movement. You use the rules to block opponents and open a path for yourself. Later levels let you stack several rules at once, which leads to some messy, surprising situations.

**What's new:** Rules aren't fixed constraints you play around. They're your main weapon.

**Development difficulty:** 4/10. A first version needs one arena, a handful of simple rules, and opponents that follow fixed patterns.

### 2. Broken Message

**Inspired by:** Broken Telephone, where a message gets twisted as it passes from person to person.

**The game:** A puzzle game. You have to send an important message through a chain of characters, and each one garbles it in its own way. Say you need to tell a robot "two steps right, one step up." One character flips directions, another forgets numbers, and a third swaps commands around. You choose the order the message travels through the chain, and you can add markers or spend a few limited corrections. At the end the robot does whatever it actually received. Your goal is to get it to the target even though the message keeps changing on the way.

**What's new:** You don't just spot the errors. You plan around them and use them to end up with the command you wanted.

**Development difficulty:** 3/10. The characters are just text and command transformation rules, so no heavy graphics are needed.

### 3. Hot & Cold

**Inspired by:** Hot and Cold and treasure hunts, where you find a hidden object from partial hints.

**The game:** An exploration game. Treasure is hidden somewhere on a map and you can't see it. Instead of direct hints, you get a limited number of detectors to place around the map. Each one only tells you roughly how far the treasure is: near, medium, or far. You combine readings from several detectors to work out where it's hiding, then decide when it's worth digging. In some levels the treasure moves after a few turns. In others, fake treasures throw off your readings. The goal is to find it in as few tries as possible.

**What's new:** Instead of searching at random, you plan your measurements and piece the clues together.

**Development difficulty:** 3/10. The first version only needs a 2D map, detectors, and distance calculations.

## Question 4 – Educational Games

Three topics that a game can teach without players feeling like they're in a lesson.

### 1. EcoCity

**Topic:** sustainability, the environment, and managing resources.

**The game:** A city-building strategy game in the spirit of SimCity and Cities: Skylines. You start with a small town and a tight budget, and you want to grow it into a big, thriving city without wrecking the environment. You build neighborhoods, factories, power plants, roads, and parks, and you have to balance what the economy needs against what each decision does to the environment. A coal plant gives you cheap power but fills the air with smog. Solar costs more but keeps the air clean.

**How the learning fits in:** You learn by living with the results of your choices. If air pollution climbs, residents get unhappy and start moving out. If you invest in public transport and green spaces, quality of life goes up. Nobody explains why balancing growth and the environment matters. You just see it happen to your city.

### 2. Shakshuka Tycoon

**Topic:** economics, budgeting, taxes, and running a business.

**The game:** A business sim. You open a small shakshuka restaurant with very little starting money and try to turn it into a profitable business. Unlike most restaurant games, you don't cook anything. The game is all about the money side: setting prices, buying ingredients, managing stock, hiring staff, and paying salaries, rent, and taxes. Things keep happening along the way, like ingredient prices going up, fewer customers walking in, a new competitor down the street, or a surprise bill. You have to keep the place running and growing without sinking into debt.

**How the learning fits in:** Every business decision has a trade-off. Raise the price of a shakshuka and you make more on each plate, but fewer people come in. Lower it and you get more customers but thinner margins. You also have to plan ahead so there's enough cash left for salaries, rent, and taxes at the end of the month. Along the way you pick up income, expenses, profit and loss, cash flow, and taxes, all by running the place yourself.

### 3. Secret Agent

**Topic:** encryption, information security, logical thinking, and problem solving.

**The game:** An adventure and puzzle game. You play a secret agent sent on missions around the world to steal secret intel, crack encrypted messages, and break into secure facilities. Each level throws different challenges at you: cracking codes, opening safes, finding patterns in messages, and solving encryption-based puzzles. For example, you might find an encrypted note that you need to decode to get a door code or the location of your next mission. As you progress, the puzzles get harder, and new ciphers, time limits, and clues to collect come into play.

**How the learning fits in:** The puzzles are the missions, so you can't progress without solving them. To crack a message you might need to spot repeating letters or use a key you found earlier in the level. Later on you run into methods like the Caesar cipher and substitution ciphers, and you learn how information gets hidden and how to uncover it. It all happens through exploring, trial and error, and moving the story forward, not through theory.
